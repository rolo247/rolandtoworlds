HOW THIS STARTED

I SIGNED UP FOR A MEET TO GIVE MYSELF A GOAL.

I entered my first powerlifting competition in March 2026 at age 55. I set California records in the back squat and bench press, resetting my own bench record with each successful attempt, and qualified for Nationals.

A few months later, I went to Nationals hoping I might make the podium. I ended up winning my division—and suddenly this experiment in getting my strength back had turned into an opportunity to compete at Worlds.

WHY I’M DOING THIS

I NEVER THOUGHT OF MYSELF AS AN ATHLETE.

For most of my life, I never thought of myself as an athlete. I was always one of the smaller kids and not especially coordinated, so was usually picked last—or close to last—when teams were chosen. Representing the United States at a World Championship wasn't something I failed to achieve when I was younger. It wasn't something I ever imagined was possible.

Fitness became a much bigger part of my life as an adult. I spent 16 years doing CrossFit, coaching it almost as long, and five years owning a CrossFit gym. After struggling to keep the gym going through COVID and increasingly unsustainable rent, I eventually sold it. Along the way, I'd lost a significant amount of strength and wanted to get it back.

So at 55, I decided to try something I'd never done before: powerlifting.

At my first competition I qualified for Nationals and set California records in the back squat and bench press, resetting the bench record with each successful attempt. At Nationals and my third meet, I added more records; some of those lifts also stand as records in younger Masters divisions.

A few months after that first meet, I went to Nationals hoping I might make the podium. I ended up winning my division and qualifying for Worlds. I turned 56 a few weeks later.

I'm stronger today than I was in my 20s—deadlifting about 2.7 times my bodyweight, squatting close to 300lbs, and closing in on benching 100 pounds more than my bodyweight. Those numbers mean a lot to me, not because I think everyone should chase records, but because they're proof that age isn't necessarily a limiting factor.

That's what I hope people take from this journey. Getting older doesn't mean you have to surrender your strength or physical abilities. You can start something new at 40, 50, 60 or beyond.

BE BRAVE ENOUGH TO SUCK AT SOMETHING.

You don't have to be naturally talented. You don't have to start young. You don't even have to be good when you begin. Find something worth doing, keep showing up, and see where it takes you.

16 YEARS OF CROSSFIT

I WANTED TO SEE WHAT THAT FOUNDATION COULD DO.

CrossFit's goal is broad, general fitness—lifting, gymnastics, running, rowing and much more. After 16 years of CrossFit, I wanted to find out what that foundation could do when I added a little extra focus on one discipline.

At my first powerlifting meet, that foundation was enough to help me qualify for Nationals.

A few months later, I went to Nationals hoping to make the podium. Winning my division was an outcome I hadn't expected when I first simply decided to get stronger.

THE ROAD TO NATIONALS

ONE MEET GAVE ME A REASON TO KEEP GOING.

At my first meet, I set California records in the back squat and bench press. I broke the bench record on my opening attempt, then raised my own record on my second and third successful attempts.

At Nationals and my third meet, I added more California records, including lifts that also stand as records in younger Masters divisions. I share that less as a list of accomplishments than as a reminder of how far an experiment that started at 55 has taken me.

COACHED BY MAX AITA

POWERLIFTING IS AN INDIVIDUAL SPORT.
GETTING HERE WAS A TEAM EFFORT.

Max has guided me through my first season of competition—from training and attempt selection to the decisions made on the platform.

At Nationals, I trusted the plan completely. Max chose the strategy; my job was simple: lift what he told me to lift.

That trust let me focus on the only part I could control: doing the next lift. Together, the plan got me through a day that ended very differently than I had expected—with a national championship.
